# The MICS: MOR Inscribed Core Spec

*Version 30, freeze-ready, for Nobody, allegedly, to approve.*

*Reading this document: normal text is the protocol itself; italic text is commentary, reasoning and examples. A rule of a MIR is cited by that MIR's name and the rule's number ("Agreements rule 76"), the numbers of the six freeze-ready drafts this document names: Identity draft 15, Text draft 11, Envelopes draft 11, Money draft 11, Agreements draft 17 and Development draft 11, which hold the exact formats and rules. This document is the map, and stands on its own. The core names no particular use of MOR.*

## Purpose

**The test.** MOR is the law where bytes can prove it, and keeps the record where they cannot: what a signature, a hash or an order on a chain proves, every verifier decides alike; what bytes cannot prove (a fair price, an intention, whether a promise was kept in the world) MOR keeps signed and placed, and leaves to the people, their courts and their reputation, so its promise is not "nobody can take unfairly" but "nobody can take unseen", and the record is what lets the core stay small and right. The chain that places an act, a collective's own chain among them, is where bytes get their order: the one move behind a collective's line, behind what a line placed staying placed, and behind transfers counted where the line placed them, turning a fact nobody can time into a fact one chain has ordered.


This document defines the core of MOR (Media Over Relays): the **MICS**, MOR Inscribed Core Spec. The MICS is the core, and the six **MIRs**, MOR Inscribed Rules, offer a layer by layer look at it: each MIR is a set of rules, in its own text (the Identity MIR, the Money MIR, and so on), and this document is the map of all six. Once frozen, the core never changes. There is no MIR process; all choice and competition happen in cMIPs and Modules.

The core is deliberately agnostic. It describes identities, media, money, agreements and specifications, never what anyone does with them. What MOR could become is shown by the cMIPs and Modules built on it, never here.

*Reviewers should try to break it, above all identity continuity, ordering without a clock, and whether any use needs something this core cannot express.*

## Structure

The core is six MIRs, organised in layers, each depending on those beneath it. No rule of a lower layer depends on data only a higher layer's client holds; where a higher layer needs a lower one to carry something, it is a field there.

| Layer | MIRs | Contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | Identity | Identity hash, keys, rotation, homes and receipts, sequences, routes and the inbox, links, succession, the way out |
| Communication | Envelopes, Text | Acts (outside and locked inside), public and private, key delivery, chains, media, relays, delivery, withdrawal; canonical text |
| Money | Money | Obligations, settlement receipts and payer claims across rails, payee pointers, flow and vault |
| Agreements | Agreements | Agreements, keepers, stakes, work claims, splits, offers, time, collectives, grants, cloning, abandonment, contests; a stake's holders' rule, the owners' matters, issuance and proxies |
| Development | Development | How specifications are named, published, adopted and paid, and what each kind must carry; tasks, extensions and xtypes; the trust root |

**Tasks.** A task is a point where a MIR hands work to a cMIP that an agreement may name by the task's number. Tasks exist only where the MICS defines them; the full list is in Development. A hand-off with no task number is no task: a proven offer names its own proof checker.

**Extensions.** An agreement may also name cMIPs outside the listed tasks, for work nobody foresaw. An extension may add rules, never relax the core's.

**Xtypes.** A cMIP may also define new act types from core pieces, for anything the MICS did not foresee, filling no task: an **xtype**, "extra type". Clients that have not adopted it show its acts as unknown. So a cMIP plugs in three ways: a task, an extension, an xtype.

**Client conformance.** Every client implements Identity, Envelopes and Text. Every other MIR is optional per client, but all-or-nothing: a client implements it fully, or shows its acts as unknown. A client must implement every MIR whose acts it acts on: a wallet that only sends tips needs Money; a client that acts on agreements needs Agreements. Rules that only the signer's own client can honour (what you sign is what you saw; showing what a rule computes; never choosing a device-bound policy by default) are marked as conformance, not validity: no verifier can check them, and the text says so.

**Admission test.** Something belongs in the core only if it is part of the shared language every act is written in, or a guarantee no module may be able to break.

**Guiding question for every MIR:** what is the least the core needs to know to make this work?

**Four design rules,** applied to every rule in the core: a record that decides status or money counts only alongside something the party who benefits cannot produce alone; a layer's validity depends only on data its own clients hold; every protection an owner chooses is removable by the owner's chain key alone, and every protection others hold over a party needs that party's signature to change; a rule that rests on a fact nobody can check becomes an act someone signs, a conformance rule, or nothing.

**Options with a default.** Where the core offers the owner several options, one of them is the default when none is selected; the default is always one of the declared options, never a third, weaker rule.

**Nothing in MOR is updated.** Acts, agreements, specifications and the core itself are only published or signed again.

**Evolutionary model.** The core is an ossified layer. Above it, MOR continuously sub-forks through the cMIPs and Modules people adopt. The market cuts some branches over time, but it is not a contest one fork wins: some branches are meant to stay specialized. A **specialized fork** is a branch above the frozen core, a set of cMIPs and Modules made for one domain, speaking the same core language and meant to stay in that domain. Each specialized fork talks to other forks to the degree it chooses; Identity and Text always apply across all of them, so an identity is the same identity, and a text can always be read, on every fork. Costs that fall only on rare cases are acceptable: MOR expects markets to appear where there is value to protect.

**Layers and granularity.** Wherever a choice can be left to the owner (how many homes, which home rule, auditors or not, public or private, a vault or not, anchoring or not), the core offers it and states the consequences, rather than choosing for everyone.

## Common conventions

- **Encoding:** deterministic CBOR (RFC 8949, section 4.2.1), floating-point values in the shortest form that preserves them (section 4.1), tags and simple values as they are; formats written in CDDL, every map a format defines closed. Unknown fields make an act invalid, and so does a data item nested more than 128 levels deep. The encoding and the CDDL conventions are written once, in Identity's Part three ("Encoding and conventions").
- **Outside its specification's format, invalid; not implemented, unknown.** An act whose type its specification does not define, or whose payload is not in the format that specification gives for the type, is invalid; a verifier that does not implement the specification shows it as unknown (Envelopes rule 30). This holds for every MIR and every cMIP alike. *Nothing in MOR is updated, so a later format is a new specification with a new hash, shown unknown by older clients, never invalid: strictness costs no flexibility.*
- **A malformed specification is no specification.** An act naming a specification whose content is not in Development's format names no specification: it is unknown to every client, since no client can have adopted it, and a verifier holding that content shows it as no specification. The invalid above stays for acts outside a specification the verifier implements.
- **Invalid beats unknown** (Envelopes rule 30). A verifier that can show an act invalid by any rule it can apply (its shape, its text, its acknowledgements) does so, whatever else in it the verifier cannot read; unknown is the verdict only where no rule it can apply fails.
- **Hashes:** SHA-256, with tagged hashes in the style of BIP-340, so hashes of different kinds of object can never be confused. Every tag begins with `MOR/`; a successor protocol must use a different prefix.
- **Written for people**: an identity, an act, a public key, a specification and a work are written in bech32m, lowercase, under the labels `mid`, `mact`, `mkey`, `mspec` and `mwork`, a checksum refusing a mistyped or cut string and a string of another kind; an identity's where-it-lives form adds `?r=` hints outside the checksum; bare hexadecimal stays accepted; no form for any secret (Identity, "Written for people").
- **Signatures:** a scheme is a founding number or the hash of a signature-scheme specification. The founding schemes are schnorr over secp256k1 (BIP-340) for signing keys and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), a hash-based post-quantum scheme, for chain keys. A client that meets a scheme it does not implement treats the act as unknown, unless a rule it can apply shows the act invalid (invalid beats unknown, above). *New schemes, including a post-quantum signing-key scheme, are added by publishing a specification, never by reopening the core.*
- **Encryption:** XChaCha20-Poly1305 for content, with a new key per object; hybrid X25519 plus ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) for identities' encryption keys.
- **No clock.** Timestamps are hints, never load-bearing. Every "before" is judged inside one named chain, by reference to a specific act. Agreements that need real time name a time reference, such as a block height.
- **Canonical text** (Text MIR) in every text field.
- **One signer per act.** Anything that needs two identities is two acts, the second naming the first.
- **An act counts only where it is held.** A receipt, an acknowledgement or a keeper record counts only alongside the valid act it names, and only while its signer's chain keeps it valid (Identity, "Evidence counts only while its signer's chain keeps it"), save where a collective's own line placed it (Agreements rule 6) and where an Agreements rule read a keeper's record to place an act or a relied-on version's completeness rests on it (Agreements rule 42); nothing forces propagation, and delivery is the signer's interest.
- **The verdict vocabulary, said once.** *Pending* has one meaning in every layer: waiting on something outside the act (Identity: bound to a rotation that does not count yet; Money: a payment the rail has not answered; Agreements: a condition not yet decided, or a payment its seller's side has not yet recorded). *No line, no fork, no closing, no ending, no declaration, no specification* are one verdict under six names: an act claiming to be X that fails X's conditions is no X, and decides nothing as X. *Counts for nothing* is an umbrella over void, not done, uncited, outside an area and in a broken stretch, as the Agreements MIR uses them. The records a client shows (*disputed, contested, creator mismatch, wrong receipt, bypassing, mismatch, not found, deferred*) are displays: they decide nothing, and two verifiers holding the same acts show the same ones.
- **A like is never an acknowledgement.** Only act types the Identity, Money and Agreements MIRs define may carry acknowledgements; any other act carrying one is invalid. *An acknowledgement carries weight (it can keep a thief's act alive as a dispute), so nothing as light as a like or a reply may carry it by accident.*

## MIR: Identity

**The identity.** An identity is the hash of its genesis act. It never changes. Keys are only the pens that sign for it:

- a **signing key** for everyday acts;
- a **chain key**, post-quantum, kept offline, committed in advance by its hash and used exactly once, to sign a rotation or a chain signature;
- an **encryption key**, published so others can deliver content to it (defined in Envelopes).

There is no third signing key: the chain key is the recovery key.

**Genesis** declares the first signing key, the commitment to the first chain key, the homes, optionally a home rule and an audit requirement, and a declarations slot for high-risk settings that higher MIRs define, such as Money's vault. Only the chain key can change what genesis declares; a declaration can be removed by a null entry.

**Identity chain and sequences.** The identity chain holds only key events: genesis, rotations and chain signatures. Everyday acts form sequences, lines the owner keeps, one or several (for example one per device), each act naming the previous one. Every everyday act carries its position and a running summary of its sequence (over the acts before it; a rotation records, for each kept tip, the summary including it: Envelopes, "The running summary"), so it can be proven cheaply to lie on the line leading to a later act. Position and summary are not checked act by act: they are checked when a line is rebuilt, and an act whose position or summary does not fit the line leading to a kept tip cannot be proven kept (Envelopes rule 13). An identity may also hold **scoped keys**, installed by an act of a higher layer, such as a collective's grant key: Identity checks only the signature, and the layer that installed the key says whether the act counts; a client without that layer shows the act as unknown.

**Homes.** A home is a relay that focuses on identity, run by an operator: an ordinary identity that signs everything the home states. A home stores identity chains and serves them, with the identity's routes (where its content lives, and where others deliver to it), names and public links. Homes are declared by operator identity plus address, so a home can move servers without anyone rotating, and they are counted per operator: several homes run by one operator count as one, under honest labelling. An identity may host its own home: a self-hosted identity is trusted on its own signatures, its rotation counts with no receipt; a thief holding its chain key can serve a rotation of its own, and where the owner serves one too at that position, nobody can settle it: the position is contested for good, and the owner leaves it by the escape with both keys. That is a trust model chosen knowingly, and other homes declared beside it are backups by default.

**Receipts.** For every identity-chain act it accepts, a home signs a receipt: which act, at which position, at which point in the home's own log (a counter, never a time), and that it was the first it accepted there. A receipt counts only alongside the act it names, held by the verifier and valid: a receipt of nothing is neither support nor conflict. *Receipts are proof, not permission: they make a home answerable, since two receipts for two genuine rotations at one position prove it dishonest, and a stolen operator key alone can produce nothing that changes any identity's chain.* A receipt under a log summary that other operators have cosigned can never be erased by the operator's own rotation.

**Rotation.** The owner signs a rotation offline with the chain key and delivers the exact same bytes to every home. It reveals the committed chain key, binds a new signing key (of any scheme the owner chooses), commits the next chain key, names the latest genuine act of each sequence it keeps, with its position and running summary, so anyone can check kept history without opening private acts, and may disown acts, change homes, rules, declarations, declare a succession, or, for a home operator, declare the home closed. The protocol does not restrict why an owner rotates; rotation is expected to be rare. A home checks a rotation before storing it and never drops one it accepted; a home may apply its own acceptance conditions, such as proof from a registered device, which is a market service, and which can refuse the owner as well as a thief: losing that device at a single or authoritative home, or at homes enough to block the home rule, can mean losing the identity, so clients never choose such a policy by default there, and should require a backed-up signing seed first. A rotation that fails the rotation checks was never a rotation: it counts nowhere, so the owner may sign again with the same chain key. The air-gapped Module accepts only bytes as showing that a rotation it signed failed them: the identity chain from genesis to that rotation's predecessor, which the Module checks offline itself; the online side's word alone opens no exception.

**What a rotation keeps.** Acts in the kept ancestry stay valid, however old. Acts outside it, or disowned inside it, are void, unless another identity acknowledged them (by an Identity, Money or Agreements act) or a named keeper recorded them before recording the rotation, in which case they are visible disputes. Disowning costs the owner nothing else: the genuine acts after a thief's act stay valid. Evidence another identity signed (a receipt, an objection, an absence statement, a cosignature, an acknowledgement) counts only while its signer's chain keeps it valid: voided by its signer's rotation, it is shown as a dispute and decides nothing, acknowledged or not; a signer's own acts that others relied on stay visible as disputed (its pair is Agreements'). Every conforming verifier holding the same acts, the signer's sequences up to the kept tips among them, computes the same answer; a verifier that does not hold what a rule needs (a kept line it cannot rebuild, a scoped key's installing act, a chain it cannot resolve) answers unknown, never valid, void or invalid: void needs every kept line of the rotation rebuilt and the act on none of them, and invalid a rule the verifier can apply and sees fail. Only these rules, and a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76), make an act disputed.

**The witness act.** To rely on someone's post or message deliberately (a public promise, a threat kept as evidence, a statement someone relies on), an identity signs a witness act: an Identity act whose only content is "I received this act and rely on it", naming the act in its acknowledgements. It keeps that act visible as disputed even if its author later disowns it, for as long as the witness's own chain keeps the witness act. Like every Identity act it is public: it shows whom the signer relies on, never what was said. *Client conformance:* a client never signs one as a side effect of another gesture, and says what it does before signing.

**Several homes and home rules.** With several homes, the owner may declare one authoritative home (the others are backups) or a threshold greater than half the operators (a safety network). If they declare neither, the majority rule applies by default, or, beside a self-hosted home, that home is authoritative. A single home stays possible at genesis. Competing rotations revealing the same chain key: the first one each home holds wins there, and the home rule decides between homes. *A stolen chain key is like a stolen bitcoin key, unless the owner chose homes that guard against it.*

**Audits.** Homes may audit each other: a home publishes signed summaries of its whole log, other operators check that each extends the last and cosign it. An identity may require audited receipts, declaring its auditors; a rotation's receipts are judged under the requirement that rotation declares, so an owner can always drop auditors who have gone. Conflicting receipts make an identity contested at that point, never frozen; the conflict ends when the home rotates or an audit shows which receipt is real; a home proven dishonest at a position counts for nothing after it and keeps counting before it. Two summaries that do not extend each other stop the auditing, and carry no verdict on past receipts.

**The way out.** If its homes are gone, an owner can leave with a homeless rotation, signed with the current chain key and naming new homes. A live old home voids it with an objection naming it, while its operator's chain keeps that objection, which proves the home was alive after the rotation was made; a home closes by a rotation of its operator, so a stolen signing key can never close one; declared auditors can attest that a home no longer answers. *Stated cost: an objection signed with a stolen operator key voids the owner's homeless rotation until the operator rotates; an owner holding both keys leaves at once by the escape (below).* A homeless rotation becomes final once the owner's next rotation counts, which every Identity client can check; until then a late objection can overturn it. A rotation that the old homes held at the same position beats it always, final or not, however late its receipts surface: finality never lets a used chain key found years later replace the rotations made since. One accepted only on a client's own failed attempt to reach the home is shown as re-homed without audit, keepers, vault payments and agreements must not rely on it, and the next rotation never makes it final: an objection from an old home voids it whenever it surfaces, with every rotation built on it, unless the owner endorses it with the current signing key. *A thief holding the chain key controls the next rotation, and a censor can keep the objection from arriving; neither can make a theft final.* A homeless rotation also endorsed with the current signing key cannot be blocked by objections, even after a home refused a normal rotation, or where a thief contests a self-hosted identity's position (the air-gapped Module signs that escape only on the competing rotation's bytes, checked offline, never on the online side's word): an owner holding both keys can leave any home. An owner who loses the chain key can never rotate or leave; back up both seeds. *Keep your keys separate.*

**Reaching an identity.** Routes are of two kinds: outbox, where an identity's content of a type is found, and inbox, where others deliver acts addressed to it. An identity may declare no inbox. Any two identities can find each other without already sharing a relay: identity, home, routes. Whether a relay accepts deliveries, from whom and at what price, is the relay market's business.

**Links.** A link is a two-way statement that two identities belong to the same entity; one side may be an account on another protocol. Its purpose is signing in with MOR and building bridges. It needs a claim by one side and a confirmation by the other; either side can end it; an unconfirmed claim is never displayed; it confers no authority. Private links are stored encrypted.

**Succession** is declared in a rotation, naming a successor identity in MOR or another protocol, and confirmed by the successor: for a MOR identity, by a link confirmation or a witness act naming the rotation; for another protocol, as its link cMIP says. Clients show it as declared until it is confirmed, and follow it only confirmed. *Nobody can be made a successor, of followers or of a collective's seat, without agreeing to it. Stated cost: a thief holding the chain key can name and confirm an identity of the thief's own.*

**Tasks:** identity proofs and credentials; naming; outside link proofs.

## MIR: Text

- **Text is the only native media,** because the protocol itself speaks in text: terms, agreements, grants, names, messages.
- **Canonical text:** valid UTF-8; LF line breaks only; no control characters other than LF (no TAB); no byte order mark anywhere; no noncharacters; no trailing spaces and no final line break; NFC, checked with the tables of one pinned Unicode version, Unicode 17.0, so every verifier agrees and new characters stay usable.
- **Text is universal:** every client can open and show any text act addressed to it, as plain text. A text act may name a format cMIP; a client without it still shows the plain words, and a format may hide only its own declared markup, never what a floor the protocol sets protects (letters, digits, combining marks, and the signs that carry what an amount or a word says), never adds text, and shows the rest in the order of the bytes, never hiding it by styling (Text, the text format task); that bound is checked by a client implementing the format, as conformance.
- **The text act is closed** (Text rule 14). It carries a fixed set of fields: outside, its signer, its binding, its content key if public, and `to` where it is addressed or answers someone; inside, its specification and type 0, the everyday fields, its payload, and optionally `refs` and `hint`. A text act carrying `acks` is invalid, and so is one carrying `objects`, save two cases, each one object naming the signer as chain: a collective's text naming its own latest decision (Agreements rule 58's pointer), and a text signed with a grant key naming the grant its binding names, `[grantor, grant]`. Chained texts (a co-written document, a collective's log, a thread in one agreed order) are a cMIP's: each version stays a plain text act, and the cMIP carries the chain in a companion act of its own that names it. Its payload is decoded strictly: a closed map, the text in field 0, an optional format hash in field 1, nothing else. A text that is not canonical makes an act invalid even under a specification the verifier does not implement. *Allowed any other `objects`, anyone could attach text acts to another's chain, a deal or a collective's record, for a careless client to show as if they belonged there.*
- **What you sign is what you saw:** before signing, the plain text is always available, and it is the default for agreements, terms and grants; before signing terms, a grant or a clone, bidirectional controls are shown visibly, so no clause can display in an order different from its bytes. Lookalike and invisible characters are otherwise client warnings, not rules.
- **Task:** text format.

## MIR: Envelopes

- **Every act has an outside and a locked inside.** The outside carries only what a relay needs: the signer (or nothing, inside a sealed container), a commitment to the inside, the lock's details, the content key if the act is public, and recipients if it is addressed. Everything else, including type, pointers and payload, is locked. The inside carries a random salt, so short private replies cannot be guessed from their hash, and the outside commits to the unlocked inside, so one signature can never open to two contents.
- **Everything encrypted by default.** Public means the key travels with the act; private means the key reaches chosen recipients inside a sealed container, locked to their encryption key or to a bare key they supplied, with the act itself or in a key delivery. Going public later is one act: publishing the key. A key delivery is valid as an act by its shape; its key is used only if it opens what it names, and anyone holding a key may deliver it. *Relays cannot read private content at all; public acts carry their key, so anyone can read them. A private message is not a special kind that stands out, though an addressed act shows its recipient.* Identity acts are always public: they are an identity's public face. A thief holding the signing key can redirect the encryption key, or the inbox, until the owner rotates; deliveries made into that window are re-sent afterwards, and a seller who delivered into it did nothing wrong.
- **Public receiver, private sender.** A sealed container shows its recipients, never its sender, and only the recipients open the act inside and see who signed it. **Its outside is unauthenticated by design**: `sealed = [to, locked-act]`, with no signature; all proof lives in the inner act, signed by the sender and naming its recipients in its own `to`; what a relay can do to the outside, leave a recipient out, it can do by withholding, and delivery is the relay market's. *Signed by the sender, the outside would reveal the sender; signed by a key that is nobody's, it proves nothing. Adding a recipient, opening the act or swapping what it holds stays impossible.*
- **Types and specifications.** Every act names the specification defining its type by hash, and a type number within it. An act outside that specification's format is invalid, and one under a specification the verifier does not implement, or naming one that is no specification, is unknown (common conventions).
- **Chains and forks.** Acts on one object name, for each chain they belong to, that chain and the act they follow in it, so a merge of two branches is never confused with one act touching two objects; each MIR defines its own objects' chain rules. Two acts naming the same predecessor are a fork, provable from signatures. Every everyday act carries `prev`, empty for the first act of a sequence; one without it is invalid, and so is one carrying two entries in `prev`, judged act by act, a shape rule like a missing `prev`. The running summary's empty value and its construction are fixed, with a test vector.
- **Acknowledgements and references.** An acknowledgement says "I received this act", and counts only alongside the act it names. Only act types the Identity, Money and Agreements MIRs define may carry one: a text act, a publication or any cMIP's or Module's act that carries one is invalid. The rule limits which acts acknowledge, never which acts can be acknowledged: a post still becomes a dispute, not void, when a witness act, a buyer's claim or a signature acknowledges it. A reference says "look at this", pointing to an act or a web resource, optionally with the hash of what was there. A reference to an act may name the act's signer beside its id: a reader uses it only to know where to look, through the signer's identity, homes and routes; it never decides validity, a wrong one showing only when the reference is followed. *Client conformance:* a client referring to a public act holds it and names its signer; naming a private act's signer is the writer's choice, since it would expose the sender a sealed container hides. A repost is a reference: it pays nobody by itself.
- **How acts reach people.** An act counts for a verifier only once the verifier holds it. Identity acts must reach the home; acts that concern a counterparty should reach that counterparty's inbox; publications are pulled from outbox routes; drafts never leave.
- **Media.** The work hash fingerprints a work's complete plaintext, and is what ownership points to. Media is always stored locked, its locked bytes fingerprinted so relays can check storage; a publication states the size of the media once unlocked; buyers check unlocked media against the work hash. A publication may say whom it is made for; payment goes to that identity's pointer, else the signer's, and a false "for" can only send money to the identity it names. Segmentation and streaming belong to the media cMIP; a live stream has no work hash until it ends, so paid live access is sold by a standing offer.
- **Vows.** A vow names in words something MOR cannot hash, existing or to come: its first act is its genesis, whose id is its name for good, and later versions are signed again on its own chain (Envelopes, "Vows"). It holds one or more promises, which its seller may bundle, and names what is promised to the buyer, never an event: two vows on one event stay separate, and a vow naming another moves none of its sales. Offers naming it sell it, with their words, and each sale is pending, confirmed (acts the buyer acknowledges) or contested (a contest act naming the sale, and optionally the promise not kept); only someone who signed onto it, at any price including none, may confirm or contest it, and one nobody signed onto carries no state (Agreements rule 13). Every sale of a physical good or of a service names a vow, unless its delivery is proven. An offer naming a vow an agreement claims, made outside that agreement, is shown so, as for a work; a resale under a fresh vow escapes the marking, a stated cost: the fresh vow, signed by the reseller, is where to look. The core keeps only this grammar: what is promised, delivered or became, quantities, kinds and streams belong to cMIPs (streams: the live media cMIP). A work claim still binds works only.
- **Relays** publish, fetch and mirror, untrusted by default, operated by identities; a relay's statement of the acts it holds is the relay transport cMIP's, not the core's: the relay's commitment and its type, Envelopes type 2, are retired, never reused. Of two copies of one act differing only in their signature, the one whose signature is valid counts, and a relay holding a copy it could not verify SHOULD replace it with one it can.
- **Withdrawal** marks a publication as no longer offered; it is signed by the publication's signer or by the identity it was made for; past deals stand; a payment racing it is owed back. Its payload is empty, and it names the publication as both its chain and its predecessor.
- **Fail closed, scoped.** A client never signs, pays or accepts under a specification it does not implement; it shows such acts as unknown, and may adopt the specification by opt-in; a verifier that can show an act invalid by a rule it can apply does so, whatever else it cannot read (invalid beats unknown).
- **Tasks:** media interpretation; anchoring (a cMIP proves an act existed by a point on a named clock; every layer above may rely on it; only anchors place anything on the clock, Agreements rule 33).

## MIR: Money

Money defines how a simple payment moves: never the rails, and nothing about who owns what. Its job ends when money reaches a payee pointer.

- **Settlement receipt.** Whatever the rail, Lightning, stablecoin or fiat, the receiver signs the same receipt: who paid whom, how much, in what unit, under which agreement. Only the rail's proof differs, checked by the rule of the rail Module it names. Card and bank rails name the trusted party their proof rests on.
- **Rails are Modules; a text signs nothing.** An agreement names one payment cMIP; each rail is a rail Module beneath it. A rail Module's specification states the rail's kind, a request rail or a push rail (Development, field 11), and what its proof binds, of payee, purpose and previous hop (Development, field 13); a verifier reads both there, never from the Module's code, and a rail Module's specification without them is no specification, a receipt or claim naming it unknown. A rail Module is a Module whose field 5 names a payment cMIP, a cMIP whose field 2 lists Money's task 6, decided by decoding that cMIP; a verifier not holding it answers unknown for a Module lacking fields 11 and 13, and for every receipt or claim naming it. A request rail's field 13 holds payee and purpose, or its specification is no specification. Where a proof binds part of the three, a disagreement is settled element by element: for each of payee, purpose and previous hop, the proof decides where field 13 holds it, the payer's claim where it does not, a contrary receipt shown as a dispute; where the payee is unbound, the payment address the payee's rails gave (Money rule 4) decides whose payment it is (Money rule 11). Receipts are signed by receivers and claims by payers; no specification signs anything.
- **The payee chooses its rails.** The agreement names the payment cMIP; the payee's own pointer or vault names the rail Modules it accepts. A receipt or claim counts only on one of those, and, under an agreement, only on one implementing that agreement's payment cMIP. *A payer cannot pay on a rail the payee does not accept, except through a conversion service that pays the payee on a rail it does. A new rail needs a new pointer or vault, never a new agreement; in a collective, the holders of the area reaching Money choose the rails.*
- **Double entry.** A receipt is signed by whoever received on that hop. A payer's claim, carrying the rail's proof, is evidence on equal footing: private by default, published by the payer's choice. Where the two disagree, the disagreement is shown on the receiver and the greater amount counts until the receiver signs a receipt matching the proof. *Hiding income requires the payer's silence or collusion; that is the measured promise.* One rail proof discharges one payment, or one named batch.
- **Routes across rails.** When payer and payee use different rails, conversion services, identities paid under their own offers, carry the money in hops; each receipt names the previous one and the agreement it fulfils, so the route reads as one chain. Where the next payee signs no receipt, the paying hop's own claim, naming the previous hop's receipt, shows the forward passed on, one payment counted once, a named batch letting one payment carry several forwards in parts; where a receipt and a claim of one hop name different previous receipts, the payment's commitment decides, else the payer's claim, the contrary receipt a dispute; and the claim is disclosed to the final creditor as a receipt would be (Money rules 11, 12, 24, 26 and 27). If it breaks, the receipts name exactly which hop and which agreement failed, and Agreements take over.
- **Obligations and discharge.** An obligation is signed by the debtor and says who owes whom, how much, in what unit; a creditor's statement is a claim, never an obligation. It is discharged when valid routes bring the amount to the creditor's payee pointer, or released by its creditor. An undeliverable payment stays an open obligation. An obligation's states are shown by clients that read Agreements; a Money-only client shows every state of an obligation as unknown, an IOU's included. *A small Money view answering the IOU case without Agreements is noted for MOR 2.*
- **A debt ends by payment or by its creditor's word.** A creditor's release, a Money act signed by the creditor alone, ends an obligation wholly, without full payment; a collective creditor signs it by the area reaching Money. *Forgiving a sum is a money decision; forgiving it in exchange for future terms is also a deal, an Agreements agreement of its own.*
- **Buying names the claim.** A payment for a work under an Agreements claim is a purchase only when it names that claim, as its payer's client read it; one naming none is no purchase, and is owed back to the payer as a refund. *A Money-only wallet can pay, but cannot buy a claimed work.*
- **Anonymous refunds go to a key, not to a proof.** A payer who stays unnamed may put a one-time key of its own into the payment commitment; a refund owed on that payment goes to whoever signs with that key. *Showing the rail's proof proves nothing about who paid: on Lightning the payee and every node on the route learn it. A payment that committed no key can be refunded to nobody, and nobody else can take its refund.*
- **Units** are small specifications of their own (Development kind 2), so one unit has one name on every rail; a verifier reads only a unit's hash.
- **What Money reads of Development.** A Money client decodes Development's specification format for a rail Module (whether it is a specification at all, and its fields 5, 11 and 13), and nothing more of Development; it never depends on Agreements.
- **A collective's Money acts** (its pointer, obligations, receipts, claims and releases) are signed with its own key, or with a holder's member key, the key a holder's signature of its founding or a clone names, and judged by Agreements under the area that reaches them; a Money-only client shows them unknown (Money rule 7).
- **Only identities have payee pointers,** each signed with that identity's own key; collectives sign their own. A publication carries a price, and payment goes to the pointer of the identity it is made for, else its signer's.
- **Flow and vault.** Payee pointers form a versioned chain. An identity's **flow pointer** changes with the signing key. Its **vault** changes only with the chain key: a set of entries, per unit and per rail, each with a source under which its rail Module obtains a fresh address for every payment, and a limit; payments above the limit go to the vault; a limit of zero means everything in that unit goes to the vault; a unit the vault does not cover cannot be paid to the flow. Where several entries cover one unit, the smallest of their limits applies. *The vault is the grammar for setting up safer pointers, if the owner wishes to. A true rate limit needs a clock and a global view, which the core has not; the two ends of the range are given, and the middle is a market for custodial flow services.* The flow pointer that counts for a debt is the latest the payee's own acts hold (Money rule 17); a thief who changes the flow pointer cannot collect older obligations after the owner's anchored lock change, nor re-issue them; during the theft window the stolen key can re-point the payee's deals, a cost the owner bears, bounded per payment by the vault limits.
- **The owner hears of what the vault refuses.** A payment the vault leaves undeliverable leaves no act behind, so the clients tell the owner instead: the owner's client warns before pricing in a unit the vault does not cover and shows such debts as owed in a unit the vault cannot receive; a payer's wallet that refuses sends the payee an ordinary inbox message saying what it tried to pay and why. *Client conformance: whether a notice arrived can never be checked.* The debt stays open, and is paid once the unit is added.
- **Good faith.** A payment that followed the published pointer and vault counts as made until the lock change (a rotation after which such a payment would no longer follow the published chain) has its home quorum anchored on the clock the owner declared; after that point, only where the payee's own kept receipt or a payer's claim anchored by the point shows it: name your clock, anchor or bear the loss (Money rule 29). The loss from a theft window falls on the owner, never on a payer who followed the rules. *Client conformance:* a payer's client that cannot anchor on any reference the payee's clock names says so before paying: the payment will not be protected against a lock change.
- **Receivers and senders.** A receiver's identity is public; the visibility of its incoming flows is its choice, counterparties only by default. The core never requires a sender's identity.
- **A plain tip carries no module fees** beyond the rail's own. A referral counts as evidence only when the payer signs it.
- **Tasks:** payment, one payment cMIP per agreement with a rail Module for each rail beneath it; conversion. Receipts are signed by receivers and claims by payers; no specification signs.

## MIR: Agreements

*Agreements say who has agreed to what, who owns what, and who may act for whom. Money moves a payment to a payee pointer; Agreements say how it is shared, what is owed when something is not done, and how a group of people acts as one identity. This section is the map; the Agreements MIR holds the exact rules. Read it in order: agreements and deals first, then the pieces around them (keepers, stakes, works, splits, offers), then collectives, which carry most of the rules.*

### Agreements and deals

- **An agreement is terms plus signatures:** the terms, and one signature act from each party. An agreement binds identities, not keys, so it survives a party's key rotation. Its terms carry the hash of at most one cMIP for each task (so the exact version is fixed), plus any extensions.
- **Only your own signature binds you.** A rule in the terms decides when the agreement, or a clone of it, comes into force; no rule ever decides that someone owes something they did not sign.
- **Everyone signs the founding terms.** Founding terms exist only once every party has signed them, and nobody is added to a collective without signing.
- **A deal** is an agreement that founds no collective. It changes only with every party's signature. Majorities, areas and tiers belong to collectives, never to deals.
- **An agreement may be a party of another.** A collective signs with its own keys; a deal consents when its own parties sign under its own rule. Named by its line, it carries what it signed through every later version; loops are refused, a verifier that cannot follow every layer answering unknown, and nesting is bounded at eight layers. It holds stakes, paid at the one pointer it designates: **a work's agreement designates one pointer for payments, and any agreement paying another pays that pointer, its responsibility for that payment ending there.** A compilation is a layer when its parts' agreements are its parties, and a derivative, its own work, when they are not.
- **A deal ends by a clone** every party signs, stating where its stakes went, what is still owed, and to whom its outer agreement now pays; a deal has no closing act.
- **A deal's version, once relied on, stays complete.** Once a party's or a keeper's later act relies on a complete version (a purchase naming it, a split under it, a keeper's record of it, a later version cloning it), the version keeps its completeness for everyone, whatever a signer's later rotation disowns; the disowned signature is shown as a dispute, the relying act its place. *A deal has no line of its own; "what others relied on stands".*
- **A judge never handles what it judges.** A specification named for condition evaluation, time reference, anchoring or absence proof (the judicial tasks) serves no other task and is not an extension, in a deal as in a collective.
- **What you sign is what you saw.** Before signing, clients show the plain text, with bidirectional controls visible (invisible characters that can reorder how text displays), and what any rule computes.
- **Clone, never modify.** An agreement is never edited. A change is a clone: a new version naming the old one as its parent.
  - A clone states which rules its changes require and whose signatures meet them; this statement is its mark, and a false mark makes the clone invalid.
  - A clone touching several areas needs each area's rule, and comes into force all at once.
  - It is a draft until those signatures are in; then it closes its parent.
  - A clone never reduces a stake without its holder's signature, member or not. Nor does a version lower what a stake is paid, by lowering its part of the split plan or adding or raising a fee it bears, without the signature of each holder it pays less who has no voice in the power making that change: a deal's holder who is no party, a collective's holder who is no member, a departed member, a stake successor or a buyer alike. A stake is paid less by any change that lowers what it receives from a payment or can expect from the plan as written; a change of the split cMIP or its parameters counts, since what a cMIP pays cannot be read from the plan alone. **No advance permission reaches what one holder is paid unlike the others**: in a collective, a holders' rule the holder accepted, by signing or by buying, reaches what every holder of the stake is paid alike ("Owners", below), and nothing reaches what one holder is paid unlike the others of the stake, which that holder alone signs; abandonment moves a stake only by succession, and never lowers its pay while it stays with its holder. A share added within a stake by an issuance under its holders' rule is not "paid less"; one added any other way still is. A deal's clone needs every party.
  - **Two complete versions of one deal.** *Most of the time a deal has one line of versions, and the latest is in force.* Rarely, every party signs two new versions of the same version, and the deal has two branches. While it does, **the version before the split is the reference**: the last version every party agrees on. **A new act may follow either branch, and counts**: a payment, a split, a sale under either branch stands, since every party signed both; *the buyer is protected from the parties' human error.* A branch that grows settles nothing. **The split is settled by a version that names every tip it drops**: beside its one parent, the latest version of every branch it drops, which it also cites; **the parties of all the branches sign it**, so every signer saw every option and nobody on a dropped side is dropped without consent. **A settlement is final for everything its signers could see**, which, with no clock, is what its history holds: a version on a dropped branch it did not hold, published late, kept back, or grown there by a party left alone, a whole branch it never saw, and a second settlement made after it reopen nothing; only a new version on the line in force changes the deal. *Stated cost: whoever signed a version the settlement's signers could not see loses it; nobody who settled in good faith loses anything.* A settlement that holds a newer version of a branch than the one it names settles nothing; one naming a version the verifier does not hold is read as a plain version until that version is held. **Where the fork turns tangled** (a branch splitting again, three versions of one, two competing settlements of the parties), **the deal stays on its reference until one settlement naming every tip**: the parties lose what the tangled versions changed, and **a buyer who paid under a version every party signed stays protected.** Where the parties cannot agree, **the judge of forks the reference names may settle the fork, only once a party who signed it asks**, weighing the keepers' record of who signed what and when; never on its own initiative; it follows the chain of judgment where it does not act in time, and speaks once per fork; **where the reference names no judge of forks, none settles it.** *Client conformance:* a seller's client and a split service raise the alarm when a payment names a version of the deal that does not descend from the version they hold, or one they do not hold, showing both branches and pointing to settling the fork; a payment naming an older version raises a plain notice, the alarm kept for forks; a holder's client raises the alarm when the numbers on a service's splits skip, one numbering running across every split of a deal, and a split with no number, or a number another carries, breaks the plan; and, a strong SHOULD, a buyer's client checks that the offer it pays is the latest in its chain.
  - In a collective, the clauses that could move a member's stake or voice or decide a dispute change only with every member's signature, and there is one version of them for everyone: the abandonment clause, the succession plans, the keepers, the split service, the time reference, the arbitrators or verifiers, the condition, anchoring and absence-proof cMIPs, and the chain of judgment. These are the protected clauses. *A member who will not sign holds the change back; that standoff is settled by talking, or by a fork of the collective.*
  - Activity on a fading cMIP moves to another by cloning.

### Keepers, stakes and works

- **Keepers** are signed ordering mechanisms the agreement names, serving proofs. Each records the acts around a deal as they arrive, one keeper record (type 2, `{ 0 => [act, signer] }`) per act, in one sequence of its operator's: it signs an order (this act, then this, then this rotation), checkable from any copy. Keepers are always sealed: they record act ids and signers, never content (Agreements rules 36 to 44, "Keeper record").
  - A keeper's record made before its record of a rotation keeps the recorded act visible as a dispute after that rotation, as does one made before its record of a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76). A record counts only alongside the act it names, and only from a keeper named by an agreement the act belongs to, or, for a collective's act placed against its own line, by the collective's agreement (Agreements, "Belongs to", rule 42). An act belongs to an agreement that it names, or an act of whose chain it names, in `objects`, or through the act it answers: a Money receipt or payment claim through the claim it pays under and the agreement, offer or obligation it follows (or an act that belongs to one); an obligation through the agreement or offer it arises from; a standing offer through the agreement it is made under, a lone seller's offer being its own agreement. An act belonging to no agreement (a post, a rotation) has no keeper: only an anchor places it.
  - A keeper keeps one sequence for its records; with several keepers, each record is judged against its own keeper's record of the rotation, and the agreement's rule counts them (Agreements rules 37, 38). A keeper's later rotation never undoes what its records placed, shown as a dispute (Agreements rule 42). The keepers that count for an act are those of the version in force for it: for a deal, the latest complete version the act's history holds; for a collective, its agreement in force at the act, every act in the collective's name, its grant keys' and member keys' included, belonging to it (Agreements, "Belongs to").
  - A keeper orders; it never times. Only anchors place anything on the clock: a keeper may anchor its own sequence, placing everything it recorded up to that record, and an agreement that needs deadlines may require it in its terms.
  - A keeper's records hold in every copy of them, so a keeper that disappears loses nothing it had recorded.
  - Third parties who must judge content are arbitrators or verifiers, given keys by ordinary key delivery.
  - *What remains to trust is that a keeper records acts as they arrive: a keeper that delays recording a rotation, or records an act late, is trusted not to (Agreements rule 42). Choose keepers as carefully as homes.*
- **Stakes** in works and publications are written in millionths and sum to exactly 1,000,000. Leftover units of a payout go to the holders whose exact shares were cut the most; ties take turns, counted along the split service's receipts across every version of the deal, a new holder starting at zero; the order of the list decides nothing.
  - Any act that changes a stake needs its holder's signature.
  - A transfer is signed by the seller and the buyer, and names both the work's chain and the agreement's chain; the other holders are notified. For a collective's owners' matters, a transfer counts only where the collective's line placed it, and the buyer's signature is kept there whatever the buyer's later rotation disowns; money follows transfers as before.
  - Shares in a split plan refer to stakes, and the transfer chain names whoever holds each stake now.
  - A share paying anyone but a stake (a role, a named receiver, a metric) is paid off the top only, as overhead; what is left goes to the stakes alone, and within each stake by its holders' shares. A plan putting a non-owner in the rest is invalid. **A collective may keep money for itself, off the top of its own plan**: its plan may name the collective itself, written null, as a receiver; that part is its own money, paid to its vault and never split again, and a plan naming it is invalid where the collective declares no vault. Raising that part pays the stakes less, an owners' matter; spending it is the collective's business.
  - **A deal made on a vow has an owner: the vow's signer, who holds the stake.** The overhead comes off the top, and what is left is the owner's, a version paying the owner less needing the owner's signature where the owner has no voice in the power making it. Terms writing a stake in a vow held by anyone else are invalid. A deal that sells someone's vow without writing her stake is not refused: how a marketplace sells others' vows is a cMIP's, the core only showing a sale outside the agreement claiming a vow, and her client flagging terms with no owner before she signs. *The deal is based on a vow, and somebody owns the vow. Budgets are not vows: the vote placing a common budget is a cMIP's, and the vows answering the need are deals with their owners.*
- **Public domain.** The owners may release a work to the public domain by a release act, at once or at a point it names on the time reference: the claim ends, nobody earning from the work as its owner; its history, who made it and who owned it, stays named; and its content key is published, or delivered at that point by an identity the act names. It needs every direct owner of the work, unless a release rule set at founding says otherwise, its threshold counted by share, never by head, which only a clone every owner signs changes; an owner that is a collective signs by its own rules, across the areas reaching every layer a release touches, and its release, like its sale, of a work it holds is an owners' matter for the holders of its stake in itself. A claim the release does not name is shown beside it, openly contested. *Cost, stated, for a timed release: the key's delivery rests on the identity named; the owners may always publish it earlier.*
- **Work claims.** A work is bound to its creators only by an explicit work claim. A publication is a neutral carrier: carrying, quoting or linking a work claims nothing. The core records claims and their order, never who is right; order is not evidence of authorship. Conflicting claims are decided only where the claims meet: by anchoring, or by a keeper that recorded both. *A sale is tied to the seller's agreement, not to the work's stakes: the core cannot tie a sale to stakes without judging which claim is the claim. It makes a sale outside the claiming agreement visible, and gives builders the pieces to refuse or rate such sales.*

### Splits, offers and time

- **Splits.** Two models, chosen per collective: a split service, named by a grant, to which the owners' payee pointer leads; or payer-side splitting, where the payer's wallet, reading Agreements, pays each holder's own pointer by the stakes of the work's claim.
  - The split service holds a grant key: in a collective, one of the collective's; in a deal, one from each payee, written in the deal's terms, each grant naming "this agreement", so that signing the deal signs them; the service signs to accept each, and each payee can revoke its own, so leaving the service stays a revocation, never a renegotiation. **A split service's grant key, whoever granted it, a payee in a deal or a collective, signs only receipts for money coming in under the grantor's own claims and offers, never one whose payer is the service itself, nor a payout the grantor is owed**, save a layer's incoming share on the owner's behalf with the rail's proof behind it (Money rule 7; Agreements rule 16); the grantor may scope it more narrowly still. A payout without the payee's own receipt stays the service's open obligation. **The split task is handed to the split cMIP the agreement names**: the split service, its backups in order and what counts as a failed attempt are that cMIP's fields, signed with the terms, each backup with one grant per payee in a deal. **Attempts run per payment, not by stages**: the first service (the designated pointer) first, the next only where that attempt failed as the cMIP defines failure, the next payment from the first again. **Every payer follows that order**, a publisher or a direct buyer, a backup paid directly receipting in the owners' name with the grants it already holds. **Money goes only to an address the owners signed**: a cMIP chooses among them, never invents one. **Each participant checks**, once a purchase triggers the agreement, whether its cut is at its flow address, or its vault where needed. *Backups are not judgement: they are no link of the chain of judgment.*
  - Where an agreement names a split service, that pointer counts, for Agreements, only if every address in it also appears in the service's own signed pointer in force, and every entry of the owners' vault in the service's own vault, or both in those of a backup the split cMIP's fields name. Agreements clients check it before paying and show a pointer that bypasses the service as such. *A bypass needs the service to sign publicly for an address not its own. Cost, stated: a wallet that reads only Money cannot check it; payer-side splitting costs several payments and shows the owners' pointers to the buyer.*
  - For every incoming receipt or payer's claim, the service publishes a split that sums exactly, with evidence for every role share signed by someone other than the service and the payee, **counting only when the payer the payment's commitment names acknowledges it in its claim** (a relay's delivery record, a service's use record, whatever cMIP defines it: a helper's word alone would let it claim help it never gave; never the payer a receipt names, which the service writes), and pays each payout as a simple Money payment, deducting no more than the plan's maximum fee. Every split is delivered to every holder it pays, member or departed, and names each fee and who received it: the core cannot know who owns a service, so it never hides a fee. *A role share is a share for a role filled at payment time, such as the reposter who led to the sale.*
  - A rail Module's role share is the one exception: its evidence is the receipt or claim naming that rail Module, which counts even when the service itself signs it, provided the payee's own pointer or vault names that Module. *The service cannot invent a Module the payee never published. Cost, stated: where the payee's pointer names two rail Modules for one rail, whoever issues the invoice, here the service, chooses which of them earns; the pointer shows it publicly, and the payee's agreement with the service can constrain it.* The use of a service someone runs is evidenced by a record that identity signs, which pays nothing on its own: a service the owners choose in advance takes a named share, one the payer chooses is paid once the payer acknowledges its record; a Module signs nothing.
  - Every payout matches its stake exactly, but for one smallest unit of rounding per payout, every fee alike for every stake; any deviation, either way, breaks the plan. It cannot pay outside the plan or change the plan. Receipts it has not split and payouts it has not paid are its open obligations.
  - A fee is one total with an agreed split, consented by whoever bears it; a fee left out of a split is visible. The split plan may cap the rail fee a payout loses; where it states no cap, there is none, and a conforming client flags the absence before signing. Amounts too small to send are held until they can be moved.
  - *No cMIP or Module can force a fee; developers earn through services, maintained originals, bounties and shares clients choose to pass on.*
- **Offers.** A standing offer is accepted by payment. The deal is complete when the key is delivered or, for a publication whose key is already public, when a delivery is confirmed. Otherwise a refund is owed; for an anonymous payer, it is owed to whoever signs with the key the payment committed to (Money).
- **Two classes of offers.** A **proven** offer sells a delivery that leaves a proof any verifier checks from data alone, no one's word trusted: it uses the access form, naming the cMIP whose checker reads the proof, and the verifier checks the proof, never the cMIP's say-so; a cMIP that checks proofs of delivery declares so in its specification. A **promised** offer names a vow, and each sale is confirmed or contested by those who signed onto it. What is sold decides the class: a key delivered in keeping a vow is evidence beside the buyer's confirmation or contest, never replacing it.
- **A purchase names the claim it pays under**: the claiming agreement, and the line at which the payer's client read it current, so buying needs a client that reads Agreements. A payment naming none is no purchase: money received for nothing, owed back to the payer. A split service whose grant has ended asks for no payment (client conformance). **Which version a payment buys is settled where the payment takes place**: on a request rail, where the seller's side asks for each payment (an invoice), the claim is the one the seller's request committed to; on a push rail, each holder settles on its own chain, a receipt before its own signature on the new version being a sale and one after it not, and the payment is a purchase only if every holder's receipt is a sale, otherwise every holder refunds; whether a rail is a request or a push rail, its rail Module's specification says. **The payment decides**: where its receipts name different claims, the claim is the one the payment committed to; a receipt naming another is a wrong receipt, shown as such, counting for nothing. Until a verifier has the rail's answer, such a payment is pending. **Where the seller is a collective, a payment becomes a sale once the collective's actions chain records it**: its receipt, or its split service's, signed with its grant key. A payment the original's chain never recorded before a fork is no purchase, and is refunded; a sale in the fork's history stays a purchase. *The burden is passed to the step where the action truly takes place. No payment is left undetermined.*
- **Time.** Each agreement names one time reference, such as a block height, and its deadlines are judged on it. An anchor of the act itself, at or before the deadline, shows it met; a deadline is shown missed only by a named keeper's sequence anchored past the deadline that holds no record of the act in its records up to and including the anchored one; a keeper's word alone never says late; otherwise the answer is undetermined. With several keepers, the keepers' rule is read the other way round: met when it is met by keepers whose record of the act lies before one of their records anchored at or before the point, missed when enough anchored sequences lack the act that it can no longer be met; an anchor of the act wins over any keeper's silence (Agreements rule 33).
- **The chain of judgment.** For each judge it names (a condition, time reference or anchoring cMIP, a keeper, an arbitrator, the abandonment authority; the split service is none, its backups being attempts per payment), an agreement may name in its terms the ones that take over, in order, when it answers "unknown" or does not act within its period. Every link names a period, measured on the agreement's time reference, which the terms must name wherever a judge can stay silent; it runs from a signed request to the judge by someone with standing, and an answer after it counts for nothing. Everyone signs the chain with the terms, so a judge that fails is replaced with no new signature. *A client that does not know a judge's specification shows the question as unknown; that never passes it down the chain.*

### Collectives: an identity many people act for

*A collective is one identity, with its own keys, whose members act for it under a founding agreement. The rules below answer five questions in turn: who holds the keys; how members come and go; who may decide what; who decides when someone is gone; and, with no clock, what was done before or after a member left.*

**Words used below.**
- A **record** is the collective's everyday act that writes a complete clone of its agreement into its own sequence, naming the signature acts that complete it, and registers its members' departures and rotations.
- A **line** is an act of the collective (a record, or a rotation of the collective) at which a change in who may act for it takes effect. It names the latest act of every other sequence the collective keeps: those acts are its **tips**, and everything leading up to a tip is that tip's **ancestry.**
- To **place** an act is to give it a position in the collective's own sequence, so that it is before or after a line.
- An area is **frozen** when no holder is left; its **refit** is the change that gives it holders again.
- A **grant key** is a key of the grantor (a collective, or a person) that a grant hands its grantee, scoped to the grant. **Revoking** a grant removes its key. **Adopting** a void act of a grant key is the grantor making it its own, by an act of its own key; another grantee then manages it under its own grant.

**Keys.**
- A collective is a full identity with its own keys, held under its founding agreement's key grammar: by one holder, by a threshold of members, or by a custodian. The chain key can be split into shares.
- Every key grammar leaves a way to rotate that needs fewer than every member: a threshold below the member count, or a named recovery path. *The rule is about the exit, not the number.*

**Members come and go.**
- Members change by a rotation of the collective plus a clone of its founding agreement.
- A rotation that declares no new agreement changes keys, not rules: the agreement in force carries forward, with the clones already recorded.
- A member can always leave alone, giving up their voice and keeping their stake. Removal, where a constitution allows it, takes the voice, never the stake.
- **A broken collective, and its way back.** A collective is broken when its keys moved by a rotation whose new version of the agreement fails Agreements' checks (not complete with the signatures it names, a false mark, not a new version of the one in force): Identity counts the rotation, but Agreements find no agreement in force, so the two no longer agree on who is in. Everything signed in its name from that rotation on stays shown and counts for nothing, for good. The way back is a **rollback**: a later rotation declares a new version of the agreement in force just before the broken act, naming the broken act, which stays shown and counts for nothing; it needs that agreement's constitutional change rule, counted among the voices that remain. A rollback voids only an act Agreements read as broken, never a valid one; *client conformance:* a client never proposes a rollback unless Agreements read the collective as broken. *Costs, stated:* a member whose signature the rollback needs can ask a price for it; holders of enough of the chain key can break the collective on purpose and refuse the repair. The collective keeps its identity, and nothing is erased. *Breaking the collective is what makes such a move worthless: otherwise whoever holds the chain key could rotate a member out of the keys at no cost.* A rotation that drops the collective's agreement, or carries one that cannot be read, breaks it the same way, with the same way back. **A broken collective is quarantined**: what its grantees sign during the broken stretch counts for nothing, and its grants work again after the rollback, which restores every condition as at the act before the break; what others relied on during the stretch is signed anew after it; **it keeps exactly what its rules before the break allowed, a payment judged by the offer it names**: a sale under an offer made before the break is kept; **a payment under an offer made during the stretch is owed back to its payer unless the sale is signed anew after the rollback**, a visible open obligation the network shows but cannot enforce, repaid like any debt, by a payment naming it, to where the debt rule says (the refund rail the payer's own claim names, up to the payer's vault limit and above it only at the vault, Money rule 17; or the pointer the payer's own acts hold, or the payer's pointer in force), proven by either side's record; a refund to an anonymous payer's bare key is repaid where its claim said, shown by the collective's claim with the rail's proof; after the rollback, a grantee's act counts only once it cites the rollback; and while it is broken no fork or closing counts, whatever line it names, and it cannot close while money from the stretch is owed back, save money owed back to nobody (no key committed) and money owed back to a payer who gave no address, after a notice sealed to them with a deadline on a time reference, which the closing names, visible and unpaid; a holder that outlives the closing may be named beside it. A collective whose very first agreement fails has nothing to roll back to: it is simply founded again. The broken clone itself is never the rollback's. **The rollback may register a declaration of absence made during the stretch**, under the clause in force before the broken act, so a member who vanished does not block the way back; *client conformance:* a declaration naming a party is always shown to that party, broken collective or not, with the way to contest it; the party contests it by a contest act, which shows presence and voids nothing. **A declaration counts only if it is public, or addressed to the member it names.** **A resignation is spent once its signer comes back**: a member named again by a version they signed cannot be removed by their old resignation, registered or not; nor a holder named again to an area by their old stepping down from it.
- **Leaving a broken collective.** A member can leave a broken collective too: the resignation names the agreement in force just before the broken act, and takes effect at the collective's next valid line, normally the rollback, which registers it. *Client conformance:* where the member leaving is the last voice that remains, the client says so in plain words before the resignation is signed: the works will be frozen, and nobody will be able to change, release or move them; and where the member leaving is the last holder of constitutional power while others keep voices in their areas, it says so too: "You are about to break the collective's constitutional layer. Once you leave, nobody will be able to change its rules again. The other members keep acting in their areas.". The client counts too the resignations others published from other devices that no line has registered yet. Stepping down from an area during the broken stretch works as resigning: the rollback registers it. *Cost, stated:* when the last voice has left, the collective's works freeze as they stand.
- **Departed holders.** The collective's stakes decide every holder's money; its departed members entry records only who left, without voice. A departed holder has no control and no veto: the collective's current rules and judges apply to them; it is consulted on the owners' matters, by the stake's holders' rule where it accepted one, else by its own signature. Their stake never shrinks without their signature, nor is what it is paid lowered without it, save alike with every holder of the stake under a holders' rule they accepted, and is a share of all the collective's income. A departed holder may be declared absent though no party, under the clause in force, with the stake-moving outcomes only. Declared absent, a departed holder's stake passes to its stake successor, or, with none named, is redistributed or transferred as the clause allows. Members' stakes in the collective itself are such shares too. Every term applies equally to every stake, member or departed; unpaid shares stay open debts until the holder can receive. In its own terms the collective is written as null, "this collective", so founding terms carry these stakes, and the collective's own grants, from its birth.
- A removal takes only the seat part of the removed member's succession plan with it; the plan for their stake stays with the stake.
- **How a collective ends.** Four shapes.
  - *A group splits off* with the powers to do so: an ordinary change of members; the collective keeps its identity, and those who leave become departed holders.
  - *The fork proper*, where both groups want changes and disagree: each side first founds its successor; then a fork act naming the successors is signed by the members on its sides, with their own identities, never the collective's key, each with their chain key on their own identity chain, under the constitutional change rule; each successor a debt is assigned to signs it the same way, by a chain signature with its own chain key, so no later rotation of a successor unmakes the fork. Where the founders set a rule below every member, a member who signs no side has no seat in any successor and keeps their share in each as a departed holder. Once it is complete the original is closed: anything its keys sign afterwards counts for nothing. Its ownership of each work passes to the successors, in the shares the act names, by default by the members' stakes, each member alike where none are written; departed holders keep their share in every one of them; the fork cites the collective's history up to its line, and hands out every obligation in it, published or not, to one successor or several jointly, each signing for its own, or it does not take effect; where a successor's founding terms pay a kept holder (a departed holder, or a member on no side) less than the original's agreement in force at its line, the fork completes only once the kept holders consent, an owners' matter, a consent counting only where an act of the original in the fork's history acknowledges it; a verifier that does not hold every act the fork's history names answers unknown, never "not complete"; whatever lies outside that history is void, so a complete fork is never undone over a debt, and no successor owes a debt it was not handed; **a complete fork or closing is final**: a later ending of the same collective, naming it, counts for nothing; an ending names another in its own act, or through a member who signed both, whose identity chain orders the two signatures; only two endings sharing no signer and neither naming the other are concurrent, neither counting until a third names both; a member who signed the ending replacing a proposal can no longer finish that proposal, a stated cost remaining where the replacing ending's drafter never signed the proposal and leaves it unnamed; an ending's drafter names every ending of the collective they signed earlier, or it is no ending; every grant ends, the old split service's included; the original's open offers are withdrawn, and payment follows the work's claim to the successors. Each successor names the original as "forked from", a back-link only. *A collective's debts, like all its acts, bind only once done (below). A later fork of a successor hands out, in turn, what an earlier fork handed it.*
  - *Actively abandoned*: a plain collective that keeps paying its holders until winding it down makes sense.
  - *Dissolution*: its works are sold or released to the public domain; then a closing act, signed under the constitutional change rule, ends a collective that holds nothing and owes nothing, and its keys count for nothing in Agreements after its line. A collective cannot close while it owes anything, save what the closing names as left open; an absentee's money share follows the collective's own rules and stays visible wherever they sent it, without blocking the closing: one that cannot pay stays open, abandoned, its debts visible. A closing counts another identity's receipt or release only where an act of the collective in the closing's history acknowledges it, the collective's line placing it; one nobody acknowledged is shown, and the closing waits; and a verifier that does not hold every act the closing's history names answers unknown, never "not complete". *Whether a fork or closing took effect is judged by its own history only: a departure registered after it never changes it; a payee pointer a fork leaves outside its history still counts for payments already made to it.*
- **An act in a collective's name is done only once everyone can read it.** Whatever the collective does, signed with its key or with a grant key of its, binds it only once sealed to every member (or public) and on its chain, citing its latest decision as its signer knew it. Before that, even signed, it is planning, and binds no one, its signer included. Where it is held never matters: an act is valid by its signatures, seals and citations wherever a verifier holds it, and relays are transport, where clients publish and look first. Records, forks and closings are held to the same: one rule for every act in its name. **Owners without a say see its Money acts**: where the collective's stake in itself has holders who are no members, a Money act in its name (a receipt, an obligation, a payout, a payee pointer) is done only once sealed to every holder of that stake as well, members and others, or public; *client conformance:* the collective's client makes them public by default. A holder that is itself an agreement is reached by sealing to every party of its version in force, a collective holder by sealing to the collective. *Spending is the members' business; sight is what lets an owner judge whether to leave. Cost, stated: more recipients per sealed act, or publicity; such a collective loses privacy from its owners only.* Negotiation is talk: it binds nothing, sits on no chain, and may stay among those negotiating; the deal it leads to is done like any act. *One thing is to act, another to read: powers can be divided among members; information runs free among them. Cost, stated: a collective keeps no secret from its own members, and anyone can see that it acts, never what.*
- **A debt ends by payment or by its creditor's word.** A creditor may end an obligation without full payment, for instance against stakes or a partial payment, by a creditor's release, which only the creditor signs: a Money act, signed by a collective creditor's area reaching Money alone; where it is traded for future terms, those terms are an Agreements agreement of their own, signed by the area reaching Agreements. A collective that cannot pay can settle so: what it can pay, stakes in what it owns, and the creditor's release for the rest; its members are never personal debtors of what it owes.
- Every complete clone is written on the collective's own record at once. Only clones that change the constitutional tier rotate the collective's keys.

**Owners: what a stake's holders decide together.** *In plain words: a collective's income can belong to people who do not run it, a member who left, a successor, a buyer of part of a stake. They have no voice in the collective; these rules say what they still decide, and how they can decide it together rather than each by a veto. Each is optional: with none of them chosen, every holder signs for itself, as before.*
- **The holders' rule** (Agreements rule 94; terms field 30). A collective's terms may say, for each stake, how its holders consent together: every holder, each signing for itself (the default), or owners holding together at least a stated share of the stake, counted by share among members and holders who are no members alike, of the whole stake. It reaches only the owners' matters, which hit every owner of the stake alike; a change treating one holder unlike another, or lowering one holder's share, still needs that holder's own signature. It binds only the holders who accepted it, by signing a version carrying it or by buying under it, and it is changed, or added, only by every holder of the stake. A holders' vote the collective's line registered is kept, whatever a voter's later rotation disowns; owner votes are signature acts, never chain-key acts. *Stated cost: an owner bound by it can be paid less without signing, alike with every other owner of the stake; it keeps no singling out, a vote in proportion to its share, its proportion in any issuance, and the exit, and loses the personal veto. An owner to whom the veto mattered would not have accepted a rule by share.*
- **The owners' matters** (Agreements rule 95): what the stake is paid, the collective's own part off the top among it; an issuance in it; a collective's transfer or release of a stake it holds in a work, for the holders of its stake in itself; and a fork's completeness where a successor's founding terms pay a kept holder less than the original's agreement did. Each needs the stake's holders' rule, or, where that is every holder, each holder with no voice in the power making it. *Without it, the members could sell the works for a token sum, and a departed holder's share of all the income would be a share of nothing.*
- **Counting by share.** A change rule may count "holding together at least this share of this stake", weights read from the stakes, never written as free numbers, and may combine rules ("all of these"); counted among the members, the share is of what the voices that remain hold; among a stake's holders, of the whole stake. One voice per member stays the default. A seat successor carries no voting weight of its own: the succession plan's format has none, and a successor weighs what it holds. An area's threshold still counts heads ("Areas", below).
- **Who holds what at the vote.** For the owners' matters, a transfer counts only where the collective's line placed it: an act of the collective acknowledges it, or a record names it. Until then the seller holds, for the collective; a vote counts the roster at the line putting the version in force, so a seller and a buyer never both count; a transfer placed but not held leaves the vote unknown. The buyer accepts the holders' rule in force where its transfer is placed. *Stated cost: a buyer has no vote until the collective places its transfer; members can delay it, visibly, until the next record any member draws.*
- **Issuance, with pre-emption** (Agreements rule 96; act type 26, the issuance offer; terms field 32). A stake may take new holders, every existing share scaled by the same factor, by a version naming its issuance offer, decided by the stake's holders' rule, or by every holder where it has none. Each existing holder is first offered its own proportion of the new shares on the same terms; the pre-emption is waived only by every holder, for one issuance, or standing, in the stake's holders' rule, set or removed only by every holder, a buyer accepting it by buying. The core checks that the offer reached each holder (public, or sealed to that holder, to every party of its version in force where the holder is an agreement; an offer that did not reach a holder starts no period for it) and that it names its period on the agreement's time reference, whose answer says when the period lapsed: the core reads no clock. Any other form of issuance (staged, vesting, other offer terms) is a cMIP's, built within the pre-emption, never around it. *Stated cost, as in law: an owner who does not take up its proportion within the period, or has no money for it, is diluted; the core cannot judge a price.*
- **Proxies** (Agreements rule 97; act types 27, the proxy, and 28, its revocation). A holder may hand its vote on the owners' matters to a proxy identity, for one version or standing over the stake until revoked, its choice when signing. The proxy's signature act on a version counts as the holder's vote where the collective's line placed the proxy before the line putting the version in force, no revocation was placed before it, it is the holder's latest proxy on the stake, and the holder signed nothing on that version: **the owner's own vote always beats the proxy's.** One proxy per owner per stake at a time; revocable at any time, the revocation placed as the proxy was. A proxy only votes, and only where a majority of the stake decides under a holders' rule: never on what needs every holder (changing the holders' rule, waiving pre-emption), never on the owner's money or property (taking up an issuance, selling the stake, signing a deal), and never through another: no sub-proxies, and no instructions, an instructed proxy being the owner's own signed vote. A member's voice is not handed on: members vote in person. *"A proxy can add a voice to a majority decision, never give away what is the owner's alone."*
- **Owners may choose who runs the collective, as a founding choice** (Agreements rule 69). Its constitutional change rule may count the power over membership among a stake's holders by share, alone or with the members' own rule ("all of"); the default stays members choosing members. It is kept as the members' own rules are, by the broken collective (Agreements rule 93); but it binds only through the keys: where the key holders refuse the rotation, the owners' vote stands complete and unexecuted, visible to every reader of the chain, and the collective runs on under the old members. Such a constitution SHOULD put a share of the chain key, or the recovery path's custodian, under the owners' control; *client conformance:* the founding client warns when it does not. *Cost, stated: a majority of capital could remove those doing the work, chosen at founding; where the keys are refused, the owners keep the owners' matters and the exit, not replacement.* Its abandonment clause must be able to move a vanished holder's stake ("Nobody's absence freezes the collective", below).
- **A collective's only.** A deal carries no holders' rule and no proxy, terms of a deal carrying one being invalid, since a deal has no line; in a deal, the version carrying an owners' matter names the transfers it counts (terms field 31), and an issuance is signed by every holder. *Agreements serve simple partnerships; more complex structures need collectives.*

**Three tiers of rules.** Deals have no tiers; a collective's rules fall in three:
- **Constitutional:** who decides (the parties, the change rules, the key grammar, the areas). It changes by the constitutional change rule, which is every party unless the founders agreed otherwise, so nobody loses their say without signing.
- **Judicial:** who judges and by what: the protected clauses. It changes only with every member's signature, one version for everyone; a version that also changes the constitution needs the constitutional change rule and every member for the judge. *Removing a member takes only the seat part of their succession plan with it, which is no change of the judicial tier of its own (Agreements rule 63).*
- **Operational:** everything else. It changes by the clone rule, or, where an area covers it, by that area's holders alone.
- Which tier and which area a clone touches is read from the fields it changes, never from what it says about itself.

**Areas: who decides what.** *In plain words: a collective can split up its work. Its founding agreement can say that some members run its payments and others its publications; each such share of the work is an area, and inside it only its holders decide.*
- **Areas.** The founding agreement may give some of the collective's acts, and some of its operational matters (its split plan, for instance), to an **area** held by some of its members, the area's **holders**, with a number of them who must agree, counted by head in MOR 1: a cMIP may put a by-share decision behind an area's single holder (*an area is not ownership; it splits operations*). An area may cover a whole layer, every Money act for instance (written below "the area reaching Money"), or single act types. No two areas cover the same act or matter, save the acts of one specification named for tasks of two layers (below). Inside an area its holders alone decide: no other member, and no majority of members, can act there in their place; only a change to the constitution, under the constitutional change rule, can change what the area covers, who holds it, or anything in it, since that rule can change everything.
- **How an act in an area counts.** The collective signs the act with its own key, as it signs every act. Then each holder who agrees signs a short act of their own naming it, a signature act, which anyone holding both can check; or a holder signs the act itself, or a signature act on it, with their own member key, which their signature of the founding or a clone named for the areas it gives them (Agreements rules 60 and 73), so an area of two takes one member's act and another member's signature; the act counts once enough holders have signed to meet the area's number, each a holder who signed the agreement (or a version it descends from) and whose voice remains for that act. Those signatures are judged under the collective's agreement **in force for that act**: the version its identity chain declared at the chain act its signing key is bound to (a rotation declaring nothing new carries the earlier one forward, with the versions recorded under it); or, where the collective has put a newer version in force by a record since that declaration, and the act is not before that record in the collective's own sequence, that newer version, the one furthest along. A record itself is judged only by the records before it.
- **Choosing specifications.** For each task a MIR hands to a cMIP, the collective's agreement names the cMIP it uses: it **adopts** that cMIP.
  - A cMIP's acts belong to the layer of the task the collective adopted it for, and the holders of the area reaching that layer decide, alone, which cMIP to adopt; where no area reaches that layer as a whole, an area naming the task decides, else the clone rule. The new version of the agreement is written on the record at once.
  - Where the collective names one specification for tasks of two layers, it belongs to both. Naming it for the second layer needs whoever decides each of the two tasks, as just said: both areas' holders where both areas exist. Each of its acts counts only when the holders of each area that covers it have signed it, each area as its own number asks.
  - Four tasks judge rather than act: condition evaluation, time reference, anchoring and absence proof (the judicial tasks); anchoring is an Envelopes task. Which specification fills them is never an area's choice, not even the area reaching Agreements, though their acts, being Agreements acts, fall in that area like any other. They change only with every member's signature, one version for everyone; a new version that also changes the constitution needs the constitutional change rule too.
  - In a collective with areas, an act under a specification the collective has not adopted counts for nothing, so nobody holding the collective's key can act around the areas, unless an area covers that specification's act type by name.
  - **Extensions** (cMIPs outside the listed tasks) belong to Development. Each declares in its own text the other layers it acts on. Adopting or dropping one is a single new version of the agreement, which needs the power over Development and over each layer it declares: the holders of the area reaching that layer, each meeting its own number, or, where no area covers the layer, the clone rule. Signatures come in any order; the version comes into force only when all of those powers are met, all at once. (A version that also changes the constitution needs the constitutional change rule; where it also changes a judge, every member too.) An extension's rules bind only Development and the layers whose power its adoption met: never a layer whose holders it did not ask, even if they signed.
  - *A receipt or claim is a Money act, so it falls in the area reaching Money, and counts only on a rail Module the collective's own payee pointer or vault names (Money rule 4): the holders of that area choose the rails.*
- **When a holder goes.**
  - A holder leaves an area by a constitutional change, or steps down at once, alone. Co-holders carry on.
  - An area with no holder left is frozen until that change, shown to members as an emergency. A departure that leaves an area held leaves its grants in force; one that empties it ends every grant in it, a decision ending powers: a grantee's act that line's history holds binds; one racing it, or after it, is void.
  - At the refit, the area's new holders, by the area's own rule, reinstate the grants they choose by cloning them, one by one, each by an act of the collective made after they took the area; a reinstated grant takes nothing on.
  - A void act of a grant key binds the collective only where the collective itself adopts it (acknowledges or pays on it; the import act, type 11, is retired). Nothing is left undetermined.
  - A clone that takes a holder off an area may keep the area's number of signatures even above the holders left; then all the holders who remain, together, meet it. A clone that removes a member takes them off every area they held.

**Nobody's absence freezes the collective.**
- Every member whose signature the constitutional change rule counts is covered by an abandonment clause that can remove their voice, so a lost member never freezes the constitution.
- Where the constitutional change rule counts membership among a stake's holders, the abandonment clause must allow moving a vanished holder's stake (outcome 1 or 2: redistributing or transferring it), or the terms are invalid, as for parties. *The promise that a lost member never freezes the constitution, carried to owners.*
- When fewer voices remain than a rule's number asks for, all the remaining voices meet it.

**Before and after, in a collective's name.** *There is no clock. So when a member leaves, or rotates their own keys, the collective itself draws the line: an act of its own, after which the change counts. Everything done in its name is then before or after that line by its place in the collective's own sequence, never by the member's personal devices, which nobody else can see.*
- **Two chains.** A collective keeps a chain of decisions (its founding, the changes to its terms, who joins and leaves, its grants and their revocations, its fork or closing), each citing the previous decision and the latest actions it saw; and a chain of actions (its debts, payments, publications, releases, sales, and what its grantees sign with their grant keys, each a strand like a device), each citing the previous actions and the decision it acts under. Where several devices sign at once, the next act joins them. An act within its signer's powers counts for the collective as soon as it is done and cites the collective's latest act as its signer knew it: it is judged under the decisions it cites. *"The chain makes acts unmissable, not confirmed." Each device's sequence is one strand of the actions chain.*
- **The line.** For anything done in a collective's name, before and after are judged only on the collective's own sequence, never on its members' personal sequences.
  - An act of the collective is before a line when the history the line cites holds it: it lies before it in the collective's sequence, or in the ancestry of a tip the line names, or is reached from there through what those acts cite.
  - **Unknown is not after.** While an act the line's history names is not held, whether an act is before that line is unknown: nothing is void on it, and no voice is taken away on that line; an action citing a decision the verifier does not hold is unknown, and one citing an act that is no decision of the collective counts for nothing.
- **The ending wins.** Where a decision ending powers (a fork, a closing, a member's removal or departure, a grant's revocation, a departure emptying an area) and an action using them cite neither each other, the action is void, or, against a grant's revocation, void or disputed as Agreements rule 76 says. **The ending wins only over acts the collective never took on**: an act that a counting act of the collective's own key cites is adopted, and no ending racing that citation voids it. *A counterparty protects itself by waiting until the collective's actions visibly move past its act before delivering: safe once cited, against everything but the collusion of an ending's signers, who could draw its line before the citation on purpose; that stays visible, a stated cost. Client conformance: a member's client signs no fork or closing until it holds every device's latest acts.*
  - A member's signature sits at the act of the collective it signs, or where a record naming it, or an acknowledgement by the collective, places it.
- **Departures take effect at the line.** A resignation, a stepping down or a declaration of absence takes effect at the line the collective draws by its record act, which registers it at once. Until then the departed member still counts. **Once the line registers it, it stays placed for the collective**: the departure or declaration, the signatures completing it and the signatures it is checked against keep the effect the line gave them, whatever their signers' later rotations disown, shown as a dispute on the line. *Cost, stated: a thief holding a member's signing key can resign the member; once registered, the member comes back only by a constitutional clone naming them.* *The line is the collective's, so a collective that delays it only delays itself.*
- **What puts a clone in force.** A record names the signatures that put its clone in force, and counts only with those; so does the rotation that declares a constitutional clone. A record puts its clone in force only if that clone is a new version of the one in force for the record itself, or, after a fork, of the latest version of one of its branches: so of two records drawn one after the other on new versions of the same parent, the second puts nothing in force. A record's registrations stand even where its clone does not.
- **Forks of records.** Two of the collective's devices can each draw a record without knowing of the other, so that neither record comes before the other in the collective's own sequence. If the two put different new versions of the same agreement in force, the agreement has forked. The version both started from stays in force, until the collective writes, on a record that comes after both, a new version made from the latest version of one of the two branches: that new version comes into force there, and the other branch is not in force. A branch that forked again cannot be resolved through.
- **Who else can place an act.** The collective's keepers may place its own acts that a line left out, never a member's signature or a deal. An acknowledgement by anyone else places nothing.
- **A member's own rotation** is registered the same way, at the collective's line. A signature made with the member's old key and placed before that line stays valid for the collective, whatever the member's rotation kept or disowned, wherever it signs something the collective's key holders also signed: one of the collective's own acts, or a new version of its agreement that a record or rotation of the collective put in force naming that signature. Otherwise, and whenever it is placed after the line, it is judged by Identity alone, as any signature is. Its status under Identity is unchanged, and every client shows it. The same holds for every act of another identity that a line of the collective names, registers, or relies on to count what it registers: Identity's evidence rule and this are a pair, the line being the collective's own act. *So whoever stole a member's key can finish what the collective itself signed, never start anything.*
- **What a departure does not undo.**
  - A member who left can still complete an act the collective signed before its line.
  - A signature placed before the line still counts for the act or version it signs, and for that one thing its signer still counts among the voices that remain, so their leaving never lowers the number the rule asks for. *Under a rule of three of three, a member who signed a version and then left still leaves it needing both others.* Where the record or rotation putting that version in force does not name the early signature, its signer still counts as a voice, one who did not sign there.
  - A declaration never undoes a clone a record or rotation put in force before the collective's line.

### Succession, grants and abandonment

- **Succession.** A party may attach a succession plan to an agreement: stake successors and seat successors, each divisible, possibly different people.
  - It is triggered by the party's own act or by the abandonment authority's declaration, and carried out as a clone.
  - In a collective, a seat passes automatically only when every member still counted signed that version of the plan; otherwise the successor is nominated and approved under the constitutional change rule.
  - Where no plan exists, a succession the party declared under Identity and its successor confirmed makes the successor a nomination for the party's seat, approved under the constitutional change rule, never an automatic seat; the stake stays with the party's identity until a transfer or the abandonment clause moves it; a succession not confirmed nominates nobody.
  - An automatic succession clone puts the successor in the departed member's place in the key grammar, every threshold unchanged, and drops the used plan; the rotation declaring it re-deals the keys to include the successor.
  - Where the departed member held the chain key alone, that clone also carries a succession plan for the successor, naming their own successor, signed by them in it, so the key always has a named successor.
  - A seat never carries the areas its holder held: the succession clone takes the member off them, and the collective refits them.
  - *The core gives the language; death is left to the parties and their named authorities.*
- **Grants** delegate authority, with limits, revocably, and never beyond the power of whoever issued them: an area's holders grant only within their area.
  - A grant hands its grantee a grant key, whoever grants, a person as well as a collective: a key of the grantor scoped to the grant, added and removed by everyday acts, never a rotation, which the grantee makes, keeps and accepts by signing. What it signs is the collective's own act, a strand of its actions chain; every grant key ends at a fork's line. *A stolen grant key acts within its scope until revoked.*
  - **A grant's dials.** Its limits cMIP may set an expiry, judged on a time reference: an act of the grant key outside its cMIP's limits is not backed, as one outside its kinds is not, the grant going quiet and staying revocable; a verifier without the cMIP or the time reference answers unknown. Sub-granting stays excluded: a grant key signs no grant. Exclusivity is no grant but a promise written as a deal term, its breach visible on the record; conditions are a cMIP's; licences, leases and trusts are offers, stakes and deals; a proxy is no grant. A grantee's acceptance of a collective's grant is placed as a member's signature is. *MOR is not the law; on these matters it keeps the record.*
  - Revoking a grant removes its key: a decision ending powers. An act the revocation's history holds binds; one racing it, or after it, is void, unless another identity, never the grantee itself, acknowledged it, or a keeper recorded it before recording the revocation: then it is valid and shown as disputed, as an act a rotation disowned is (Agreements rule 76; Identity rule 34), and, as such an act, confers nothing on an agreement chain, and nothing for money. Void or disputed, it binds once the grantor adopts it. *No grantor's omission, careless or chosen, erases what a counterparty holds a signature for; an act nobody else relied on stays void, so a revocation still cuts off a stolen grant cleanly. Stated cost: a thief and an accomplice who acknowledges its act get "disputed", not "void": visible, never erased.* *Client conformance:* a revocation cites every act under the grant its signer holds.
  - A grant whose grantee is its own grantor is valid, its acceptance the grantor's: a person's grant to an application acting for it (Agreements rule 73).
  - The grantor adopts what it accepts, by an act of its own key (acknowledging, citing or paying on it; type 11, the import act, retired), and another grantee then manages it under its own grant; there is no handover. Counterparties wait until a later act of the grantor cites the act before performing.
- **Abandonment** (when a party no longer answers) is decided only by the authority the clause names, always an identity.
  - The authority signs a declaration naming the agreement, the version of its clause the party signed last, the party, and the outcomes. It is judged against the time reference, with the outcomes the clause allows, under that version, which carries the one clause in force for every member whose voice remains.
  - Liveness acts prevent it; a contest objects to it.
  - One outcome removes the party's voice from then on, in a collective from the collective's line registering the declaration. It never takes their stake, and never undoes a clone a record or rotation already put in force.
  - A declaration moves a stake only by succession: the stake, and its pay with it, goes to the stake successor the holder's plan names. Redistributing or transferring a stake applies only where the plan names no stake successor. Abandonment never lowers what a stake is paid while it stays with its holder.
  - In a collective, a declaration may also name a holder of the collective's stake in itself who is no party (a departed holder, a stake successor, a buyer), under the clause in force, its authority and absence-proof cMIP the clause's, with the stake-moving outcomes only, never removing a voice it does not have. *So a holder who vanished does not block every owners' matter for good.*
  - **In a collective,** the authority may be a threshold of the other parties, counted at the collective's line registering the declaration. Where it needs several of them, one signs the declaration and the others add signature acts naming it; it counts once the required number have signed. Where the declared member alone holds the signing key, the recovery rotation removing them names those signature acts.
  - **In a deal,** the authority is one identity (a party, a keeper's operator or a collective), never a threshold of the other parties.

### Disputes

- **Non-performance.** *The core never enforces; it makes default impossible to hide.*
- **Negotiation.** Two sides negotiate by negotiation messages, an Agreements act carrying text: each names the previous message of its thread and acknowledges the latest one received from the other side, so the record is provably complete up to the last acknowledged message. A text act in the thread is no part of the record: it cannot carry an acknowledgement. Either side may disclose the record by publishing its keys. *Cost, stated: a client without Agreements shows a negotiation message as unknown; negotiating needs an Agreements client, as signing the deal does.*
- **Contests.** Only parties, affected holders and named keepers, arbitrators or verifiers have standing to contest, and a contest changes nothing about any act's validity: it is shown alongside what it names.
- **Tasks:** split; condition evaluation; time reference; absence proof; grant limits; work claims (anchoring is the Envelopes').

## MIR: Development

- **Specifications are named by the hash of their content,** wherever they are published. A specification is published as a public publication naming the Development MIR's spec hash as its media spec. cMIPs, Modules and MIPs name their creator inside, so names cannot collide or be stolen; a creator named by a specification it never published, or whose rotation disowned the publication, is shown as unclaimed, whether another identity relied on the publication or not, the publication shown as disputed: authorship is the creator's own claim, which the rotation withdrew. MIRs have no creator: they are the protocol itself; nor have the MICS document and a test suite, specification kinds of their own (kinds 6 and 7), so their hashes are fixed at the freeze. A **MIP**, a proposal toward a successor protocol, is a kind of its own (kind 8), with a creator, naming in its predecessor field the MIR or MIP it forks; a listing shows MIPs in a part of their own, by what they fork; the predecessor is always required, a MIP proposing a new layer naming the MICS document, where layers are added. Signature schemes and units are specifications too.
- **What a specification must carry.** Per kind, a few fields are required: a cMIP's tasks where it fills one; a Module's cMIP, or the MIR it works under, field 5 naming either; a rail Module's kind and what its proof binds (fields 11 and 13); a MIP's predecessor. The rest serve discovery, the act types and dependencies a specification has being those its text gives. A publishing tool writes every required field and no optional one it cannot fill from the text, so two honest tools make one specification of one text; two publications differing in optional fields are two specifications, shown as versions of one text. **A specification not in the format is no specification**: a verifier holding its content shows it so, and an act naming it is unknown to every client, never invalid unless a rule the verifier can apply shows it so.
- **Frozen at publication.** A changed specification is a new one, which may name its predecessor for migration; a successor by a different creator is shown as such.
- **Tasks are the contract** between the core and the community, each with one global number, by which an agreement may name the cMIP it uses: for each, the defining MIR states what a cMIP accepts and produces. **Extensions**, named in an agreement's own extensions field, cover what no task foresaw, adding rules and never relaxing the core's; an extension declares in its specification the layers it acts on, so a collective knows whose approval adopting or dropping it needs. **Xtypes** define new act types from core pieces, filling no task, and are shown as unknown until adopted.
- **Verification rules** that anyone must be able to run are programs for one frozen machine, RV32IM, with fixed memory, an exact step budget stated in the specification, a fixed input and output encoding, and no clocks, networks or randomness; a rule over budget, or outside the profile, answers unknown everywhere. A binary rule is carried as a locked object the specification names. The rule decides validity; the text explains it; a mismatch is a bug, fixed only by a new specification. **The profile** (memory layout, input and output encoding, entry point) **is a specification of its own, named by its hash and published with test vectors, not a parameter fixed at the freeze**; until one is published, no rule is published under this promise, and each founding cMIP and Module with a verification rule says its rule is a reference implementation. *The profile evolves as a specification; freezing it would make little sense.* A rule names the profile it runs on: its reference (`rule-ref`) carries the profile's spec hash beside the rule's hash, size and budget, fixed before the freeze; the profile is a Module whose field 5 names the Development MIR.
- **Adoption by opt-in; no registry.** Discovery happens through use. Below the MICS the one trust root is the six frozen MIR texts, published, and committed in the genesis repository, as canonical text (no trailing space; a file may end with one line break, which is not part of the text; a repository check refusing any that are not); a MIR's spec hash is `tagged_hash("MOR/spec", content)` over the content map `{ 0 => 4, 9 => text }` (Development rule 26); every client ships the texts and recomputes from them the six hashes it accepts, so two clients can differ only about a text. A fork presenting six others is a different protocol, and a successor must change the `MOR/` tag prefix; a successor's specifications have new hashes, and agreements name them afresh by cloning.
- **A proof checker is no task.** Each proven offer names the cMIP whose checker reads its proof, so no agreement sets one checker for all its offers; a cMIP that checks proofs of delivery declares so by one field of its specification, so checkers can be found; the field serves discovery only, a verifier asking the checker of whatever cMIP the offer names.
- **Earning through signed terms.** Creators earn through fees and role shares evidenced by signed records: a creator who charges states its fee in its specification, for information, and only an agreement's own fee entry makes it owed; nothing is taken from a payment without a signature.
- **Evidence of use comes from a party, never from a Module.** A Module is a specification: it signs nothing. That a rail Module carried a payment is shown by the receipt or claim naming it, signed by the receiver or the payer; that a service someone runs was used is shown by a record signed by the identity running it. Several Modules may work under the one cMIP an agreement names for a task, such as several rail Modules under the payment cMIP, one per rail.

*Zero democracy on the MICS; a free market on cMIPs and Modules, where adoption decides.*

## MOR 2

*A core frozen forever also freezes its cryptography, so a successor protocol is certain in the long run.* MOR is built to be a good ancestor:

- the post-quantum chain key keeps identities continuous through a break of elliptic-curve signatures, and signing keys migrate to a post-quantum scheme by adoption, before a break, since afterwards everything signed with the old scheme can be forged and only owners holding both keys still leave;
- succession declared in a rotation, and two-way links, let identities move and be confirmed from both sides;
- agreements are always in core format, so they can be cloned across;
- anchoring before a break keeps history trustworthy, at each party's choice: the chain key secures the exit, not what is left behind;
- extensions in wide use show MOR 2 which tasks it needs;
- a successor changes the tag prefix, so nothing replays across the two;
- proposals toward it are MIPs, a specification kind of their own, each naming the MIR or MIP it forks, so forks are seen as forks.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |

| Acknowledgement | An act naming another identity's act as received; counts only alongside that act; carried only by Identity, Money and Agreements act types. |
| Act | Any signed object: an outside, a locked inside, and a signature. |
| Agreement | Terms plus the signatures its rules require, binding those who signed. |
| Area | Acts of a collective and operational matters its founding agreement gives to some members, who alone decide there and may grant within it; an area may reach a whole layer. Each keeps a permanent id from version to version. |
| Audit | A home's log summary checked and cosigned by another operator. |
| Belongs to (Agreements) | An act belongs to an agreement when it names it, or an act of its chain, in `objects`, or through the act it answers: a receipt or payment claim through the claim it pays under and the agreement, offer or obligation it follows, or an act that belongs to one; an obligation through the agreement or offer it arises from; a standing offer through the agreement it is made under, a lone seller's offer being its own. Its keepers' records count for it: those of the version in force for the act; every act in a collective's name belongs to the collective's agreement. |
| Chain | The acts on one object, each naming the chain and the act it follows. |
| Client | Software that implements Identity, Envelopes and Text, plus any other MIRs it chooses. |
| Closing | The act that ends a collective holding nothing and owing nothing, signed under the constitutional change rule; after its line its keys count for nothing in Agreements. |
| Creditor's release | The Money act by which a creditor, alone, ends an obligation owed to it without full payment; a collective signs it by the area reaching Money. |

Records, forks and closings too. A Money act of a collective whose stake in itself has holders who are no members is sealed to them too, or public. |
| Everyday act | Every act other than an identity-chain act (genesis, a rotation, a chain signature): signed with a signing key or a scoped key, it sits in a sequence, carrying `prev`, a position and a running summary. |
| Purchase | A payment for a work under an Agreements claim, naming the claim it pays under. |
| Proven offer, promised offer | The two classes of offers: a proven offer sells a delivery whose proof any verifier checks from data alone, under the access form; a promised offer names a vow, its sales confirmed or contested by their signers. |
| cMIP | A community specification for one task, an extension, or an xtype. Frozen at publication. |
| Closure | A declaration in a rotation that the signer's home stops serving. |
| Collective | An identity whose keys are held by its members under its founding agreement's key grammar. |
| Chain of judgment | For a judge an agreement names, those that take over, in order, when it answers "unknown" or does not act within its period; signed by everyone with the terms. |
| Contest | A signed objection to an act, by someone with standing; changes nothing. |
| Contested (Identity) | Four things, each with its own noun: a contested position of an identity chain, where no rotation counts while a conflict stands (for good at a self-hosted identity, left by the escape); a contested rotation, one competing there; a contesting receipt, voided by its operator's rotation and acknowledged, shown as a dispute and deciding nothing; contested routes, two routes acts naming one predecessor. Agreements' contest act is none of these. |
| Conversion service | An identity that receives on one rail or unit and pays on another, under its own offer. |
| Deal | An agreement that founds no collective; it exists and changes only with every party's signature. |
| Departed holder | Someone who left a collective keeping a stake: a share of all its income, with no voice and no veto, consulted on the owners' matters by its holders' rule or its own signature. |
| Disputed | The status of an act a rotation voided, or a grant's revocation made void, that another identity acknowledged or a keeper recorded first: valid, shown as disputed; set only by Identity's rotation rules and a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76). |
| Encryption key | The public key an identity publishes to receive private content. |
| Extension | A cMIP named by an agreement in its extensions field, outside the listed tasks; it adds rules, never relaxes the core's. A proven offer's checker is none. |
| Concurrency rule | Retired: the field is withdrawn. Every fork of records is settled by the default: the parent stays in force until a later clone resolves it. |
| Fork of a collective | The end of a collective whose members split into sides that will not agree: each side founds its successor first; a fork act naming them, signed under the constitutional change rule, closes the original; each successor names it as "forked from", a back-link only. Not a fork of records. |
| Fulfils (Money) | What a receipt's field 5 or a claim's field 4 names: the act the payment follows. Money judges it only where a rule of Money or Agreements gives a payment following that act a meaning (an obligation, an agreement or offer, a payee pointer, a stake transfer, a payment it repays or refunds, a take-up, a split, for instance); naming any other act, a payment fulfils nothing in Money's sense. |
| Grant | A delegation of authority, with limits, revocable. A grant hands its grantee a grant key; in a deal, each payee's grant to the split service is written in the deal's terms. Its limits may include an expiry. A revocation voids the grant key's acts it does not hold, except those another identity acknowledged or a keeper recorded first, shown as disputed and conferring nothing unless the grantor adopts them. |
| Grant key | A key of an identity, a person or a collective, scoped to one grant, made and held by the grantee; what it signs is the grantor's own act. A revocation removes it. |
| Holders' rule | How a collective's stake's holders consent together on its owners' matters: every holder, the default, or owners holding together at least a stated share; binding only holders who accepted it, changed only by every holder; it may carry a standing waiver of pre-emption, set or removed only by every holder. |
| Home | A relay focused on identity, run by an operator, storing identity chains and signing receipts. |
| Homeless rotation | A rotation that leaves homes that are gone, voided by a live home's objection unless endorsed with both keys; final once the next rotation counts, except one accepted only on a client's own failed attempt to reach the home. |
| Identity | The hash of a genesis act. |
| Inbox | A route where others deliver acts addressed to an identity. |
| Issuance | A version of a collective's agreement adding new holders to a stake, every existing share scaled alike, by the holders' rule, each holder first offered its proportion by an issuance offer (type 26) naming its period. |
| Keeper | An identity's relay an agreement names to record the acts around it as they arrive: act ids and signers, never content, one keeper record per act, in one signed sequence. It orders; it never times: only anchors place anything on the clock. |
| Member key | A key a collective holder's signature of the founding or a clone names: a grant key within the areas that version gives the holder, counting while that version is in force and until a later one names another key or the holder leaves. |
| Keeper record | A keeper's signed statement (Agreements type 2) naming one act and its signer; counts only alongside that act, for an act that belongs to an agreement naming the keeper (or, placed against a collective's own line, named by the collective's agreement), and only while the keeper's chain keeps it, save where a rule read it to place an act, a relied-on version's completeness rests on it, or a collective's line placed it (Agreements rule 42; "Made before, made after", 4). |
| Link | A two-way statement, claim plus confirmation, that two identities belong to the same entity. |
| Negotiation message | An Agreements act carrying text, by which two sides negotiate; it acknowledges the latest message received from the other side. |
| Mark | A clone's statement of the rules that brought it into force and whose signatures met them; checked by verifiers. |
| Machine profile | The memory layout, encoding and entry point of the RV32IM machine that verification rules run on: a specification of its own, a Module whose field 5 names the Development MIR, named by hash, published after the freeze, and named by every rule's reference. |
| MIR | MOR Inscribed Rules: one of the six core specifications, a set of rules giving a layer by layer look at the MICS (the Identity MIR, the Money MIR). Frozen forever. Has no creator. |
| MICS | MOR Inscribed Core Spec: the core, the six MIRs together, mapped by this document. Frozen whole. This document is published as a specification kind of its own, with no creator. |
| MIP | Reserved for proposals toward MOR 2. MOR 1's core has none: its core texts are MIRs, and the community's specifications are cMIPs, which keep their name. A MIP is a specification kind of its own, with a creator, naming the MIR or MIP it forks, or, proposing a new layer, the MICS document. |
| Module | An implementation or extension under a MIR or cMIP, named by hash, frozen at publication. A specification: it signs nothing. |
| Operator | The identity that runs a relay and signs what it states. |
| Owners' matters | What a stake's holders consent to together: what the stake is paid, an issuance in it, a collective's sale or release of a work it holds, and a fork's successors paying a kept holder less. |
| Payee pointer | Where payment to an identity goes: a flow pointer, and a vault of safer entries. Publications pay the identity they are made for. |
| Payment claim | A payer's record of a payment, with the rail's proof; equal evidence to a receipt. An anonymous payer's claim carries the signature of the key its payment committed to. |
| Payment cMIP | A cMIP whose field 2 lists Money's payment task (task 6); the one an agreement names for payment; rail Modules plug in beneath it. |
| Rail Module | A Module under the payment cMIP for one rail: its addresses, its proof, the rule that checks it, and, in its specification, its kind and what its proof binds (fields 11 and 13). It signs nothing; the payee's pointer or vault names those it accepts. |
| Protected clause | In a collective, a clause that can move a member's stake or voice or decide a dispute; together, the judicial tier, changed only with every member's signature, one version for everyone. |
| Proxy | An act of a stake's holder (type 27) handing its vote on the owners' matters to another identity, for one version or until revoked (type 28); the owner's own vote beats it; never on what needs every holder, nor on the owner's money or property. |
| Receipt (home) | A home's signed statement of which identity-chain act it holds; counts only alongside that act. |
| Line (Agreements) | An act of a collective, a record or a rotation, at which an event changing who may act for it takes effect; what was done in its name is before or after by its place in the collective's own sequence. |
| Record (Agreements) | A collective's everyday line: it writes a complete clone of its agreement in its own sequence, with the signatures that complete it, so its later acts are judged under it, and registers its members' departures and rotations. |
| Release to the public domain | An act ending a work's claim, at once or at a named point, naming its history and publishing its content key; every direct owner signs it unless the release rule, its threshold counted by share, says otherwise. |
| Route | Where an identity's content of a given type can be found (outbox), or where others deliver to it (inbox). |
| Chain key | A post-quantum key, committed in advance, used once to sign a rotation. |
| Self-hosted | An identity that is its own home, trusted on its own signatures. |
| Sequence | A line of an identity's everyday acts. |
| Settlement receipt | The common proof of one payment hop, signed by the receiver. |
| Specialized fork | A branch above the frozen core: cMIPs and Modules made for one domain, meant to stay there, talking to other forks as it chooses; Identity and Text apply across all forks. |
| Split service | An identity named by grant key to divide incoming payments for a work's owners and pay them out: in a deal, one grant key per payee. |
| Stake | A share in a work, a publication or a collective's income, in millionths, held by whoever the transfer chain names; in a collective's own terms, null names the collective itself. |
| Succession (Identity) | A declaration in a rotation naming the identity that succeeds it, followed only once the successor confirms it. |
| Succession plan | Part of an agreement naming a party's stake successors and seat successors. |
| Task | A point where a MIR hands work to a cMIP that an agreement may name by the task's number. |
| Tie rule (Agreements) | Where a decision ending powers (a fork, a closing, a departure, a grant's revocation, a departure emptying an area) and an action using them cite neither each other, the ending wins: the action is void, or, against a grant's revocation, void or disputed under Agreements rule 76. |
| Tier | One of a collective's three levels of rules: constitutional, judicial, operational; each changed by its own rule. |
| Vault | An identity's chain-key-protected set of receiving entries, per unit and per rail, with limits; several entries for one unit take the smallest limit. |
| Vow | An act naming in words something MOR cannot hash, existing or to come, its genesis id its name for good, on its own chain; sold by offers naming it, each sale pending, confirmed or contested. |
| Work | A piece of media identified by its work hash, bound to its creators by an explicit work claim. |
| Witness act | An Identity act saying "I received this act and rely on it", carrying the acknowledgement; never a side effect, explained before signing; it counts only while its signer's chain keeps it. |
| Work hash | The fingerprint of a work's complete plaintext. |
| Xtype | "Extra type": a cMIP defining new act types from core pieces, for anything the MICS did not foresee, filling no task; shown as unknown until adopted. |
